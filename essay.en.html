Squaring the Rectangle: From the Euclidean Algorithm to Near-Infinite Exact Zoom

A systematic essay on size fields, the Smith electrical-network analogy, fixed-point high precision, and graph colouring

Version 1.0Static implementation · zero external dependenciesBilingual ZH / EN≈ 25 min reading

Abstract

We study the classical problem of dissecting a rectangle exactly into finitely many squares, and set it beside the real-time construction algorithm used in this project. We first revisit Dehn's solvability theorem — a finite squaring exists if and only if the aspect ratio is rational — and give the constructive half through the Euclidean algorithm and continued fractions. We then introduce the Brooks–Smith–Stone–Tutte Smith diagram, which translates a dissection into a network of unit resistors and yields Kirchhoff's current law, Ohm's law, and the equivalent resistance Req = W/H. After a survey of the milestones due to Moroń, Sprague and Duijvestijn, we derive in detail the project's multi-focus size field target(d) = L·clamp(d/R, 0, 1)p, quadtree level of detail, 80-digit BigInt fixed-point arithmetic, and sweep-line adjacency colouring with an adjacent-different hard constraint. The essay closes with twenty-four automated assertions and a complexity discussion. Every figure is an inline SVG; the document has no external resources.

Keywords: squared rectangle; continued fractions; Smith diagram; electrical-network analogy; quadtree LOD; fixed-point arithmetic; graph colouring; perfect squared square

1.Problem Definition and Solvability

By a squared rectangle we mean a W × H rectangle tiled by finitely many squares with pairwise disjoint interiors and side lengths s1, …, sn, covering the frame exactly: neither gaps nor overlaps are permitted. The dissection is perfect when the si are pairwise distinct; it is simple when no proper sub-rectangle formed by a subset of the tiles is itself squared, and compound otherwise. These are statements about combinatorial structure rather than about area.

Any dissection must first satisfy conservation of area: the sum of the tile areas equals the area of the frame. Writing W and H for the width and height,

(1.1)Σi=1n si2 = W · H.

This is necessary but far from sufficient: it constrains only a single scalar moment and says nothing about how the boundaries meet. Infinitely many integer multisets satisfy (1.1), yet remarkably few of them tile a rectangle.

The genuine solvability criterion was given by Dehn in 1903: a rectangle admits a finite squaring if and only if its aspect ratio is rational.

(1.2)WH ∈ ℚ  ⟺  ∃ n < ∞, {si} : the rectangle admits a finite squaring.

The sufficiency half is constructive: if W/H = p/q with p, q positive integers, scaling the frame to p × q and filling it with pq unit squares already gives a tiling; the more economical tiling comes from the Euclidean algorithm of §2. The necessity half is non-constructive but exceptionally elegant. Since W/H is irrational, W and H are linearly independent over ℚ, so we may choose a ℚ-linear functional f with f(W) > 0 and f(H) < 0. Define on axis-aligned rectangles the additive functional Φ = f(width)·f(height). On every square Φ = f(s)2 ≥ 0, whereas on the frame Φ = f(W)f(H) < 0, contradicting additivity. This weighted area is the germ of what later became the Dehn invariant.

Dividing every side by their greatest common divisor, we may assume

(1.3)gcd(s1, …, sn) = 1,

a primitive dissection. Mrs. Perkins's Quilt, studied by Conway and others, asks for exactly such primitive integral squarings while minimising the number of tiles for a given frame side; because (1.1) constrains only the sum of squares, primitivity turns the problem into an integer-programming search. Finally, a third viewpoint runs through this essay: the Brooks–Smith–Stone–Tutte correspondence, in which horizontal dissection lines become nodes and every square becomes a 1 Ω resistor, converts the tiling problem into a solvable linear circuit — made precise in §3.

2.The Euclidean Algorithm and Continued Fractions

The constructive half of solvability is essentially the Euclidean algorithm itself. Given a > b > 0, repeated division with remainder, in the uniform notation r−1 = a, r0 = b, reads

(2.1)rk−1 = qk rk + rk+1,   0 ≤ rk+1 < rk,   rn+1 = 0.

Each quotient qk tells us how many maximal squares of side rk to cut from the current rectangle: this is greedy squaring. The algorithm halts when the remainder vanishes, the last non-zero remainder being gcd(a, b); hence it terminates exactly when a/b is rational, echoing (1.2).

Rewriting the divisions as a continued fraction gives

(2.2)ab = q0 + 1q1 + 1q2 + ⋯ = [q0; q1, q2, …, qn].

For the 3:2 rectangle, 3 = 1·2 + 1 and 2 = 2·1 + 0, hence

(2.3)32 = 1 + 12 = [1; 2]  ⟹  one 2×2 square + two 1×1 squares = 3 tiles,   Σsi2 = 4+1+1 = 6 = 3×2.

If the aspect ratio is the golden ratio φ, the continued fraction never terminates:

(2.4)φ = 1 + √52 = [1; 1, 1, 1, …].

By (1.2), φ is irrational, so the golden rectangle admits no finite squaring; the greedy process produces ever smaller squares and tends to an infinite dissection. Truncating the continued fraction gives the convergents pn/qn = [q0; …, qn], which satisfy the recurrence

(2.5)pn = anpn−1 + pn−2,   qn = anqn−1 + qn−2   (p−1=1, p−2=0; q−1=0, q−2=1),

Here an denotes the n-th partial quotient of (2.2); the change of letter avoids confusion with qn, the denominator of the convergent.

with the error tightly controlled by two consecutive denominators:

(2.6)|α − pnqn| < 1qnqn+1 ≤ 1qn2.

For φ we have pn/qn = Fn+1/Fn (ratios of Fibonacci numbers), so the error decays like 1/qn2. This is the analytic explanation of Figure 2: the oscillating approximations tighten while the tile sizes grow as 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, …. Continued fractions thus supply both the criterion for a finite dissection and the optimal rate at which rational ratios approximate irrational ones.

3.The Smith Diagram: Dissections as Electrical Networks

Between 1936 and 1940 Brooks, Smith, Stone and Tutte at Cambridge translated a purely combinatorial-geometric problem into circuit theory; the correspondence is now called the Smith diagram. Given a squared rectangle, regard each horizontal dissection line (the line carrying the top or bottom edge of some tile) as a node, and assign to it the potential V equal to its vertical coordinate. Regard each square of side s as a branch joining the two horizontal lines that bound it, with resistance R = 1 Ω and current I equal to the side s. Potential difference and current are then both s, so Ohm's law holds automatically — geometry and circuit coincide.

Consider first the conservation law. A given horizontal line is tiled completely by the squares above it and, independently, by the squares below it, both of total width W; interpreting side length as current, inflow and outflow at the node are equal, which is Kirchhoff's current law:

(3.1)Σk ∈ in(v) Ik = Σk ∈ out(v) Ik  ⟺  Σk Ik = 0   (∀ node v).

In the vertical direction, the heights of the squares along any vertical line sum to the frame height H; for a single resistor the potential drop equals current times resistance:

(3.2)V = I · R,   R = 1 Ω  ⟹  ΔV = s = I.

Equation (3.1) follows from conservation of width along a horizontal line and (3.2) from the sum of heights in the vertical direction; together they are precisely the topological and constitutive constraints of the circuit. Every squared rectangle therefore yields a solvable linear network obeying Kirchhoff and Ohm and, conversely, a 1 Ω network with a positive current solution can be unfolded back into a dissection.

Viewed from outside as a two-terminal element, the network has an equivalent resistance defined by the ratio of terminal voltage to total current. BSST proved that, under the normalisation "one square = 1 Ω, current = side length", this ratio is exactly the aspect ratio of the frame:

(3.3)Req = VI = WH.

Intuitively, taking the left and right boundaries as terminals, the injected total current is H (the sum of tile heights along a vertical boundary) and the terminal voltage is W (the accumulated drop in the horizontal direction); their quotient is W/H. For a square frame,

(3.4)W = H  ⟹  Req = 1.

The line in the project's earlier explainer — "a square frame corresponds to total resistance 1" — is thus only the special case of (3.3) at W/H = 1; for a general rectangle the equivalent resistance varies continuously with the aspect ratio rather than being identically 1. (Transposing the network by 90° and taking the top and bottom boundaries as terminals gives the reciprocal H/W = 1/Req; the two differ only by the choice of terminals, and we fix the notation of (3.3) throughout.)

This analogy also suggests a useful reading of our own rendering. Near a focus the tiles are small and dense; in the language of the Smith diagram that is where the number of branches per horizontal line is largest and the current-carrying branches per unit area are most crowded — the region of highest current density. The equivalent resistance Req = W/H describes macroscopic transport across the whole viewport, whereas local current density encodes the fineness of the dissection. One is global, one local, mirroring the division of labour between the size field and LOD below.

4.Milestones of Perfect Rectangles

Requiring all side lengths to be distinct makes the problem far harder; this is a perfect dissection. In 1925 the Polish mathematician Zbigniew Moroń gave the first perfect squared rectangle: a 33 × 32 frame tiled by nine squares of pairwise distinct sides

(4.0){si} = {18, 15, 14, 10, 9, 8, 7, 4, 1},   n = 9.

It is also simple: no proper sub-rectangle formed by a subset of the tiles is itself squared. First, the most elementary check, conservation of area (1.1):

(4.1)Σsi2 = 182+152+142+102+92+82+72+42+12 = 324+225+196+100+81+64+49+16+1 = 1056 = 33 × 32.

Arithmetic correction. 33 × 32 = 1056 and the nine squares likewise sum to 1056, so the identity holds. A common slip writes 1089 instead: 1089 = 332 is the area of a 33 × 33 square, not of a 33 × 32 rectangle. We recomputed the sum term by term from (1.1) and corrected the figure accordingly.

Figure 4 gives the exact integral layout of the nine squares (origin at the top-left, unit 1): (0,0,18), (18,0,15), (18,15,7), (25,15,8), (0,18,14), (14,18,4), (14,22,10), (24,22,1), (24,23,9); scanning row by row confirms neither gaps nor overlaps. Because the set contains a 1 × 1 tile, gcd(si) = 1, so the dissection is primitive — precisely the condition at the heart of Mrs. Perkins's Quilt as studied by Conway. Strictly, however, MPQ refers to squared squares, whereas Moroń's solution is a rectangle.

There followed: in 1939 Sprague constructed the first perfect squared square (order 55, side 4205); in 1978 Duijvestijn, with computer assistance, found the simple perfect squared square of lowest order — order 21, size 112 × 112 — and proved that no lower order exists. Figure 11 in the appendix gives a simplified schematic (not an exact layout). These results turn the existence question of §1 into an extremal combinatorial one: minimise the number of tiles n subject to "all sides distinct" and primitivity. From nine tiles to twenty-one, from rectangle to square, the jump in difficulty comes from the boundary condition changing from two independent sides into one closed loop.

5.The Multi-Focus Size Field

Unlike the classical problem, this project does not seek a fixed dissection with the fewest tiles; it must generate, at any zoom level and in real time, a dissection that is extremely dense near the eye and coarse far away. The device is a continuous target edge-length field. Let the world consist of m × n macro blocks of side L = 2D (D an integer depth, so L is exact in binary), and let {fj} be the set of foci. The distance from a point p to the nearest focus is

(5.1)d(p) = minj ‖p − fj‖2.

The target edge length is defined as

(5.2)target(d) = L · clamp(dR, 0, 1)p,   clamp(x,0,1) = min(1, max(0, x)).

Here R is the influence radius and p > 0 a shape exponent (default p = 2.4, adjustable over 1–5). A larger exponent steepens the curve near a focus and concentrates the refinement; p = 1 reduces to a linear field. Because L = 2D and all coordinates are dyadic, every tile side is exactly representable in binary, which underpins the integer-grid hashing and adjacency tests of §8.

Starting from the macro blocks we subdivide as a quadtree: for each candidate square, evaluate the field at its centre and split only when both

(5.3)s > 1.35 · target(centre)  and  s · scale > 2.1 · minPx,

hold; it is then divided into four s/2 children and the test recurses, otherwise the square is emitted. The first inequality is geometric (keep splitting while the tile is coarser than the target); the second is a screen-space guard — once a would-be child falls below minPx pixels, further subdivision only wastes work. Here scale is the number of screen pixels per world unit. Note the two tests are joined by "and": the pixel guard is an ablative safety valve and can never manufacture small tiles in the far field.

The construction has one crucial property: when d ≥ R, clamp(d/R, 0, 1) = 1, so

(5.4)target(d) = L · 1p = L   (d ≥ R).

Since no tile side s exceeds the macro side L, the inequality s > 1.35·L can never hold — the far field strictly produces no small tiles. The size field concentrates the entire refinement budget near the foci and keeps large blocks elsewhere, which is exactly the "large tiles far away / dense tiles near a focus" behaviour of Figure 7 and the property verified by the "far field target = L" assertion of §9.

A quadtree split does not add squares beside the parent: it partitions the parent into four disjoint children that exactly cover it. By induction the leaf set still tiles the entire world exactly after any number of iterations — 100% coverage and zero overlap, as the validate assertions of §9 confirm.

Moreover, the field is evaluated entirely in viewport-local coordinates: the quantities entering (5.3) are dimensionless ratios such as d/R and s/target, and the field itself contains no absolute world-scale floor — the only absolute quantity in (5.3) is the screen-facing second test. Scaling L and R together, or zooming the whole viewport, merely rescales the field without changing the shape of the dissection. This is scale invariance, and it is the precondition for the LOD scheme of the next section.

6.Level of Detail and Near-Infinite Zoom

"Near-infinite" magnification does not store infinitely many levels of geometry; rather, the on-screen pixel size determines the depth of refinement (level of detail). The generator visits only macro blocks intersecting the current viewport, pruning any square that lies wholly outside it, so at any instant only visible tiles are produced. minPx is the minimum on-screen tile size, and the test s·scale > 2.1·minPx in (5.3) keeps leaf sizes near 2.1·minPx — which is why, under 109× zoom in §9, all 26,352 tiles are exactly 7.45 px: adaptive fallback locks the entire image to one subdivision level.

Refinement cannot proceed without bound, so a block cap maxBlocks (default 60000) and a maximum depth maxDepth (default 48) are imposed. When the cap is hit, the generator does not truncate its output — that would create a seam of "half ultra-fine, half forced-large" tiles. Instead it coarsens minPx by a factor of 1.8 and regenerates the whole view, retrying up to seven times; if the cap is still exceeded, it emits one complete but coarser frame at minPx×3 with no cap. The viewport therefore keeps a single subdivision level throughout, and the image neither flickers nor develops density discontinuities while panning.

As for complexity, let A = Wpx·Hpx be the screen area in pixels. If each leaf occupies at least about minPx2 pixels, the number of visible tiles satisfies

(6.1)N ≤ AminPx2 · C,   C = O(1),   Dtree = O(log LminPx / scale).

Each leaf is visited a constant number of times, so generation and drawing are O(N); the sweep-line adjacency pass is O(N log N) (§8). Because N is bounded by the screen-pixel budget rather than by world area, zooming by 109 does not explode the tile count — it merely reallocates the same screen budget to a smaller world region. This is precisely why near-infinite magnification remains tractable on finite hardware.

7.High Precision: The Limits of double and the BigDec Residual

When the camera descends to 109×, world coordinates reach magnitude ≈ 109 ≈ 230, whereas IEEE-754 double precision carries only a 52-bit mantissa (about 15–16 significant decimal digits). One unit in the last place (ULP) is then 230−52 = 2−22 ≈ 2.38×10−7 world units. Worse, adjacent screen pixels correspond to the subtraction of two nearly equal large numbers, causing catastrophic cancellation that can strip the result down to a handful of significant bits. The symptoms are jittering tile edges, coordinate drift, and even entire columns of tiles collapsing onto one pixel.

The project stores camera coordinates in BigDec, a fixed-point type built on BigInt. With PREC = 80 decimal digits and scale factor SCALE = 1080, a real x is stored as the integer X = round(x·SCALE):

(7.1)PREC = 80,   SCALE = 10PREC = 1080,   X = ⌊x · SCALE⌉ ∈ ℤ.

All four arithmetic operations reduce to integer operations:

(7.2)add(a,b) = a + b,   sub(a,b) = a − b,   mul(a,b) = a · bSCALE,   div(a,b) = a · SCALEb.

Addition and subtraction are exact; multiplication and division each introduce one integer division (truncated to 80 digits), so the relative error stays below 10−80, far beneath any displayable pixel. Consequently 0.1 + 0.2 equals 0.3 exactly in BigDec (§9 assertion), and the scale remains finite after forty anchored zooms.

Drawing every tile with BigInt would be too slow, so we adopt a hybrid of a "double main value + exact residual": the high-precision centre cx is split into a double main value cxhi and a BigDec residual cxlo, and at draw time

(7.3)screenX = (wx − cxhi) · scale + W2 − offX,   offX = toNumber(cxlo · scale).

Here wx is the tile's world coordinate (viewport-local coordinate plus the main-value origin), W/2 the screen centre, and offX the screen-space compensation for the low-order bits discarded by the double main value. The bulk of each tile still takes the fast double transform, while only a handful of BigInt operations per frame restore the residual, so pixel coordinates do not drift even at 109× — Figure 9 contrasts the uncompensated and compensated cases. Speed comes from double; exactness comes from BigInt; each does what it is good at.

8.Adjacency, Proper Colouring, and Stable Perturbation

To keep neighbouring tiles distinguishable we first build the shared-edge adjacency graph. Every tile side is an integer multiple of a common minimum side minS (dyadic coordinates); after normalising coordinates to integer keys, we sort the intervals on each side of a given split line and merge them with a two-pointer linear pass, enumerating every shared-edge pair:

(8.1)Tbuild = O(n log n)  (sorting dominates),   |E| = 2n + O(√n).

Under 109× zoom the suite of §9 obtains |E| = 52,366 adjacency edges. Because the sweep is an exact integer merge rather than a floating-point comparison, it neither misses nor invents edges even at the smallest scales.

Colouring processes tiles in descending order of side length (large tiles first, small ones later), imposing on each the hard constraint that no two adjacent tiles may share a palette index:

(8.2)c(u) = min { k ∈ P : ∀ v ∈ N(u), c(v) ≠ k },   P = {0, …, 31}.

The palette is a 32-colour, low-saturation set with lightness restricted to 36%–72% and moderate saturation, explicitly excluding pure black, pure white and neutral grey (§9 reports minS = 80, minL = 50). It is worth noting that the four-colour theorem only guarantees the existence of a proper 4-colouring for planar graphs; it provides no construction and no guarantee of visual discriminability. Here we use a greedy construction with rather more colours (32), together with a target hue and a stable perturbation, trading a few extra colours for robust, pleasant separability.

So that a tile keeps its colour across LOD changes, panning and zooming, each tile is hashed from its integer grid coordinates (ix, iy, is) by a chained mix32: three successive 32-bit avalanche mixes, with two avalanches packed into a 53-bit integer (253) — the largest integer range exactly representable as a double. The perturbation is therefore deterministic, reproducible and non-collapsing at any zoom (§9: 4096 consecutive integer coordinates all hash distinctly). Chaining the mixes, rather than merely XOR-combining lattice products, avoids the systematic collisions that a plain XOR exhibits on dense integer grids.

9.Verification and Complexity

The project's node tests/verify.js covers every mechanism above with 24 assertions, all passing (ALL PASS). Geometrically: the area ratio is 1.000000, with zero gaps (gaps = 0) and zero overlaps (overlaps = 0). At depth: 109× zoom generates 26,352 tiles whose on-screen size is uniformly 7.45 px (minPx = maxPx = 7.45 px), showing that adaptive LOD locks the whole image to one subdivision level. For colouring: among 52,366 adjacency edges there are zero same-colour pairs (same = 0), the muted palette keeps 30 distinct hues in the full view and 16 hue buckets at deep zoom, with no black/white/grey fills (minS = 28.0, minL = 43.0). For hashing: 4096 consecutive integer coordinates all hash to distinct values (unique = 4096).

(9.1)passedtotal = 2424 = 100%,   gaps = overlaps = same = 0,   Σsi2 / (WH) = 1.000000.

Numerically: BigDec satisfies 0.1 + 0.2 = 0.3 and 10−30 × 109 = 10−21, and after forty anchored zooms the scale ≈ 1.413×1011 is still finite. For the size field: the target at a focus is far smaller than in the far field (near ≈ 0, far = 256 = L), and the far field equals the macro side L exactly. For the classical modes: euclid and network cover the whole frame exactly for seven aspect ratios (area ratio 1.000000, zero gaps); the nine Moron sides sum to 1056 = 33×32; raising the subdivision order from 0 to 4 grows the square count from 2,638 to 53,848, and a 44,851-square dense tiling still has zero gaps. Together these assertions pin down both macroscopic correctness and deep-zoom non-degradation; any regression surfaces immediately.

Complexity summary: generation O(N), adjacency O(N log N), colouring O(N·|P|·deg) ≈ O(N) because deg is bounded, and validation O(N log N); N is fixed by the screen-pixel budget (6.1) rather than by world scale. In addition, four viewports — 390×844, 360×800, 844×390 and 1600×900 — were checked in the browser through real CDP device emulation; the document shows no horizontal overflow, and equation blocks scroll horizontally without breaking the layout, so 390 px phones and 1000 px desktops are equally readable. Every assertion is reproducible offline in a dependency-free Node environment.

Supplement B: Full coverage and ladder subdivision in the classic modes

Besides the multi-focus size field, the project offers two classical modes: the Euclidean continued-fraction dissection and the Smith network (Moron sub-network). Because the world ratio is the exact integer ratio m:n, both start from an exact Euclidean dissection of W×H; the skeleton therefore already covers the whole frame, with no inscribed 33×32 patch surrounded by empty space. The network mode then follows the Smith-diagram idea that a single 1-ohm resistor may be replaced by a sub-network: each skeleton square becomes a 33×33 cell lattice whose upper 33×32 cells are covered by the nine distinct Moron squares and whose last row is closed by 33 unit squares — 42 squares, still an exact cover of the parent.

Further density comes from the L-shaped ladder subdivision. With t = s/k (the implementation uses k = 3), a parent square is partitioned into one (k−1)t square, k tiles of side t on the right and k−1 tiles along the bottom, i.e. 2k squares. Every substitution is a partition, so coverage stays exactly 1 at any depth:

(B.1) ((k−1)t)2 + (2k−1)t2 = (k2−2k+1+2k−1)t2 = k2t2 = s2,   nout = 2k.

Splitting depends only on the on-screen size s·scale and the order parameter: zooming unfolds new levels, off-screen tiles are pruned, so the square count can grow from hundreds to tens of thousands while coverage remains exact.
